London, 1836. The entire city runs on a kind of silver-working magic. Silver bars can produce all sorts of effects — making carriages run faster, helping fishing boats catch more fish, or even serving as medicine. Each bar has two different languages engraved on its two faces, and the bar manifests the untranslatable gap between them.

But the silver bar alone isn't enough — someone fluent enough in both languages has to speak each engraved word aloud at the bar for the magic to take effect. "Fluent enough" means dreaming in that language.

That kind of language specialist is trained at Oxford's Babel — the Royal Institute of Translation. Through the British Empire's vast colonial reach, Babel's scholars gather languages from around the world, study their grammar and etymology, and use that research to create new silver-working magic.

The protagonist, Robin, is a boy who grew up in Canton. His father, Lovell (though he vehemently denies Robin is his son), is a scholar at Babel specializing in Chinese. Because the Qing Empire explicitly forbade teaching Chinese to foreigners, the only way to learn it was to custom-raise a child in China. Robin grows up in Canton, and only once his Chinese — and the English he's additionally taught — is fluent enough does Lovell bring him back to England.

Lovell hires tutors for Robin to learn Latin and Greek — the entrance requirements for Babel, since so much of the silver-working magic in current use is implemented pairing English with vocabulary from these two languages. After a brutal period of intensive tutoring, Robin reaches the threshold to be admitted without examination, and enters Babel. There, he meets his close friends: Ramy, from Calcutta, India; Letty, daughter of a British admiral; and Victoire, of Haitian descent.

---

My first impression reading this book was "Harry Potter for language majors" — both stories are set in some kind of British academy full of people in robes. But look closer, and Harry Potter is closer to a middle or vocational school: there's a fixed curriculum, and the material is very hands-on. Casting spells, flying brooms, brewing potions — the focus is on repeated practice so you can apply it in daily life (or literal "combat"). Exams are like standardized tests, scored numerically; teachers hold absolute authority; the school's conflicts revolve around friendship, house rivalries, romance, sports, and rule-breaking. Students who are academically strong and students who aren't can coexist in the same story just fine.

Babel, by contrast, feels much closer to graduate-school humanities culture. Students write reams of translation-theory papers, debate etymology, and produce original silver-working spell pairings. Their relationship with professors is more like a mentorship, carrying real academic dependency. They discuss where knowledge comes from, and who benefits from it. Exams are pass/fail qualifying exams requiring an oral defense; those who fail are expelled in extremely dramatic, humiliating fashion. Everyone inside Babel is a language prodigy and a top student. But across the whole of Oxford, outside Babel there are plenty of wealthy students who spend all day drinking, writing poetry, and rowing.

Even though both books depict British campus life, Babel has none of Harry Potter's joy.

Harry Potter himself is a well-known figure in the wizarding world — his troubles are mostly about being too famous: plenty of people who like him, plenty who dislike him, but that's a "good problem" to have. Babel's protagonists, by contrast, are foreigners and women struggling to survive in an academic world dominated by white men, and the entire book is steeped in racism and sexism. In the early-to-mid part of the book, Robin is also a fairly passive character, mostly pushed along by events, passively surviving them. Plot-wise, it's not an easy, breezy read.

The author, R.F. Kuang, was once asked in an interview to describe the book in three words, and chose "historical, dark, academic." The story is set with genuine historical precision in 1836, with real historical figures and events woven together with fictional characters.

---

"Dark academia" is a genre I only learned about after finishing this book. The characters in the book study in a kind of gothic, shadowy academy, poring over their books almost obsessively in pursuit of knowledge. The protagonists are crammed with reading material and assignments under Babel's high-pressure environment — during major exams they get dark circles under their eyes, they throw up; they dig so deep into etymology that they feel like they can no longer speak their own mother tongue, or make simple daily small talk. As Robin mentally prepares himself to face death, the figures his mind turns to are Socrates and Seneca.

The author seems particularly skilled at capturing the atmosphere of academia — she can write a genuinely captivating lecture scene, and renders the psychological state of academic pressure in fine detail. And the author has no intention of letting the reader stay a comfortable bystander: across 600 dense pages, packed with footnotes, historical background, etymology, and translation-theory trivia, readers are meant to endure the same grinding study and discrimination alongside the protagonists.

From a speculative-fiction design standpoint, the silver-working magic system is genuinely clever — it cleverly fuses etymology and translation theory into a distinctive fantasy mechanic, while staying tightly bound to imperialism, colonialism, and the role academic institutions play in both.

---

Taken purely as entertainment, this novel holds up reasonably well. But if you try to seriously unpack its political implications, I find some of it unsettling — not the deliberately dark atmosphere the book cultivates, but the values reflected in the book's overall tone.

Robin does endure racism, having his very existence denied, and being treated as less than human, from beginning to end. All of that accumulates until he becomes deeply willing to resort to violence — and he scales his own personal humiliation up into a framework of imperialism and colonialism as a whole.

**Spoilers follow —**

Robin eventually storms Babel with a gun, demanding the faculty and students "strike" or leave. The book frames this action as a "strike," on the grounds that the entire British Empire's daily operations depend on the silver-working magic that Babel students routinely maintain; if maintenance stops, carriages and ships stop running, bridges collapse. They try to use this to force Parliament to halt its opium war against the Qing Empire.

But a strike, in the ordinary sense, is workers self-organizing and voluntarily withholding their own labor — it shouldn't involve violently coercing other people into compliance. Robin's action is much closer to a hijacking, a bank robbery, or an armed uprising — and in a modern context, it's hard not to also think of American school shootings.

Earlier in the book, Robin steals silver bars and grammar books from Babel for a secret society, on the grounds that this knowledge originated from imperialist and colonial exploitation, yet was never given back to those it was extracted from — especially his own "homeland." And yet the book's portrayal of the Qing Empire throughout is fairly one-sided, largely filtered through a British lens. The book mentions Britain publicly displaying the bound feet of Chinese women, but its depiction of Canton amounts to little more than one bleak scene in an opium den and one brief, fairly flattering meeting with Lin Zexu. Robin never sees the Qing Empire's own tyranny and oppression, and yet he's still fully willing to engage in espionage and violence on its behalf. Read today, it's hard not to think of China's "Thousand Talents Plan" academic-espionage controversies.

What unsettles me is this: could this be a microcosm of some mainstream strand of thought in Anglo-American academia? Having read extensively in colonial theory, you can certainly come to understand the evils of colonialism and the suffering of the colonized. But when it comes to actually acting on that understanding, are these people genuinely willing to use violence against a relatively rights-respecting empire, in support of a far more tyrannical homeland?

Ironically, even though this book holds a fairly romanticized image of the Qing Empire, it was still disqualified from the 2023 Hugo Awards in Chengdu through an opaque process (apparently for political reasons). I wonder what the author took away from that irony.
